    Building a square wave out of sine waves.

Sources:
J. Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822).
The square wave is (4/π) times the sum over odd k of sin(kx)/k.
Only odd k appears because the square wave has half-wave symmetry: f(x+π) = −f(x), and every even harmonic repeats over that shift instead of flipping.
The 9% overshoot that will not go away at the jumps is the Gibbs phenomenon: J. W. Gibbs, Nature 59, 606 (1899).

Sources:
G. Galperin, "Playing pool with pi (the number pi from a billiard point of view)", Regular and Chaotic Dynamics 8(4), 375-394 (2003).
Simulation is event-driven and exact: perfectly elastic collisions conserving both momentum and kinetic energy, mass ratio 10,000:1, giving exactly floor(100*pi) = 314 collisions.
The count equals floor(pi / arctan(sqrt(m/M))), which is why the digits of pi appear as the mass ratio grows by factors of 100.
In the coordinates (sqrt(m1)*v1, sqrt(m2)*v2) shown in the video, conservation of energy confines the state to a circle and each collision is a reflection; every pair of collisions rotates it by 2*arctan(sqrt(m1/m2)).

    How many times does the coin turn?

    STEM · geometry · 3 panels

    Panel 1 Panel 2 Panel 3
    Caption 
    A coin rolls all the way around an identical coin, without slipping. How many full turns does it make?

Almost everyone says one. The two edges are exactly the same length, so one lap should be one turn.

It makes two. The centre of the rolling coin traces a circle of radius 2r, so it travels 4πr, while the coin's own edge is only 2πr long. Twice the distance is two full turns.

This is the coin rotation paradox. It cost SAT test-writers a public correction in 1982, when the official answer key said three and every option was wrong.
